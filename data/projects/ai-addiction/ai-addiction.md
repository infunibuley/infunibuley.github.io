### Human-AI Decision-Making & Cognitive Biases

*Steyvers, M. and Kumar, A. (2023) “Three challenges for AI-Assisted decision-making,” Perspectives on Psychological Science, 19(5). Available at: https://doi.org/10.1177/17456916231181102.*

Steyvers, M. and Kumar, A. (2023) focus on specific challenges of AI-Assisted decision making. The first challenge is understanding "the conditions that support complementarity" or, in other words, whether human-AI decision making is superior to human-human or AI-AI decision making at all. The second is understanding the expectations and the understanding of strategies that could be used that humans have of AI. The third is the understanding of "different design choices for human-AI interaction," including usage strategies and timing. 

For the first challenge, Steyvers, M. and Kumar, A. (2023) look at Steyvers et al. (2022), where Steyvers et al. finds that human-AI can surpass either human-human or AI-AI decision making. I am curious if their human-human testing involved two people who are already comfortable with each other or know each other or if the people are completely unknown to each other. 

For the second challenge, Steyvers, M. and Kumar, A. (2023) find that there is a difference in preference on whether to rely on AI or humans for different scenarios. for "**highly consequential scenarios** (Castelo et al., 2019)", there's a preference to rely on humans, but for situations with a "**high perceived degree of objectivity**" and "**low-stakes quantitative tasks**", a there is a preference for AI rather than humans (Steyvers, M. and Kumar, A. (2023) refers to Castelo et al., 2019). I do wonder if there were only two options: human or AI, or if it was more open ended to include internet searches or dictionaries or other information gathering methods. 

Steyvers, M. and Kumar, A. (2023) mentions results reported by Tejeda et al. (2022). These results show a difference in reliance strategy based on "**DMs’ own confidence state**, the **confidence expressed by the AI**, and overall AI performance". These findings suggest that "individuals’ appraisal of others is profoundly shaped by their own perceived capacities", but individuals' evaluations of AI are significantly different from their own self-appraisals. 

For the third challenge, Steyvers, M. and Kumar, A. (2023) focus on the different strategies for using AI advice in human-AI decision making. They look at **different paradigms of AI usage, concurrent, sequential, on demand, and time delayed**. 

In the concurrent paradigms, the problem is introduced alongside the AI advice. 

In the sequential paradigm, the human decision maker is first presented the opportunity, then makes their own independent decision, and then only receives the AI advice. Some research shows that the sequential paradigm improves decision making accuracy for "encouraging independent reflection" (Green & Chen, 2019), but other studies found no differences in performance (Buçinca et al., 2021; Tejeda et al., 2022). 

The on-demand paradigm allows individuals to choose when to use AI advice, but there were no improvements found in overall accuracy. 

The time-delayed paradigm "delays the provision of AI advice", improving accuracy. Similar to the sequential paradigm, a possible explanation could be in the individuals' "additional time to reflect on the problem" that this paradigm offers. "Rastogi et al. (2022) showed that people are more likely to detect AI errors when more time is made available" to reflect on the AI prediction. 

Another part Steyvers, M. and Kumar, A. (2023) look at is the AI's confidence and explanations and how that impacts the individuals' decision making. Individuals were more likely to follow the AI's advice based on higher confidence. They consider that human-AI decision making might improve even more in accuracy if the AI is able to provide precise explanations with their predictions to help discern flawed reasoning or hallucinations. Bansal, Wu, et al. (2021) showed that with the technique of "feature attributions" in retrieving **explanations** regarding AI-predictions did not improve performance compared to only the AI's indication of confidence, or even just **increased individuals' trust in the AI decisions** regardless of accuracy of the advice. Steyvers, M. and Kumar, A. (2023) mentions the need for more research on how to increase the cognitive engagement of the individuals (Gajos and Mamykina, 2022) or to design behavioral interventions (Buçinca et al., 2021). 

Before this reading, I hadn't exactly considered the difference in decisions between **high or low consequence tasks and scenarios**, so I will be discerning those in the survey and analysis. I'll also probably look at user's confidence levels on the tasks with and without using AI or the internet. I also hadn't considered the effects of different paradigms, and I'm still not sure if I want to focus on **which paradigms people tend to use** or which paradigms are better. I'm leaning towards the former because I want to focus on individuals' behavior with AI rather than improvements or concerns about the effectiveness of human-AI interaction.

### Cognitive Costs, Fatigue, & Impact on Human Skills

*Musazade, N., Mezei, J. and Wang, X. (2025) “Exploring the impact of AI tools on cognitive skills: A comparative analysis,” Algorithms, 18(10), p. 631. Available at: https://doi.org/10.3390/a18100631.*

*An actual experiment concerning deskilling across three cognitive dimensions. *

Musazade, N., Mezei, J. and Wang, X. (2025) focus on the "impact of Generative AI (Artificial Intelligence) algorithms on human decision making in complex problem-solving tasks", focusing on 3 different cognitive components: **analytical, creative, and systems thinking**, as well as their subskills. They consider different definitions of the cognitive components and attempt to test student participants to learn how different kinds of GenAI use and non-use affect the decision making differently. 

#### Analytical thinking: 

Their subskills: breaking down concepts and ideas; information evaluation; argument evaluation; questioning; decision making; inductive and deductive inference; interpretation; logical thinking; identifying alternatives; formulating hypotheses; rejecting unsupported conclusions.

#### Creative thinking:
There were a couple different contrasting definitions of creative thinking:

- “capacity to bring a new idea or concept into existence through imagination and to imagine something that does not exist”
- "reasoning that uses imagination to substitute, expand, modify, or transform the symbols, images, ideas, patterns, conditions, or elements in the world around us."
- "novelty and appropriateness"

Their subskills: bringing up new ideas; novelty; appropriateness; goal and problem definition and representation; divergent thinking strategy; finding analogies; combining information; seeing the big picture.

#### Systems thinking: 

The main definition used for systems thinking was the "capacity to understand how concepts work together, embrace a multidisciplinary approach, and identify patterns over time."

Their subskills: relationships between concepts; multidisciplinary perspective; identifying patterns over time; applying multiple perspectives; holistic approach; mental modeling; recognizing system boundaries; identifying and quantifying system components; recognizing and characterizing interconnectedness; predicting future behavior.

#### Their findings: 

Overall, AI usage supported "logical reasoning, structuring, and problem definition", but "showed weaknesses in novel idea generation, multidisciplinary integration, and critical rejection of unsupported conclusions." They do mention that their definitions and research criteria could be improved, assessment models could be improved, and their sample size was small.
